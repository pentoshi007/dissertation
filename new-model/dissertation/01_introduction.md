# Chapter 1: Introduction

India is one of the world's largest importers of crude oil. More than 80 per cent of the country's petroleum requirement is met through imports, and these imports are priced in United States dollars on global commodity markets (Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas, 2023). Consequently, any movement in the international price of crude oil, or in the INR/USD exchange rate, translates directly into a change in the rupee cost of imported crude. This makes India's domestic price level inherently sensitive to what may be called the rupee oil price—the product of the dollar-denominated Brent crude price and the prevailing INR/USD exchange rate.

A large body of literature has examined the relationship between global oil prices and macroeconomic outcomes in India. However, many of these studies focus on headline consumer price inflation (CPI) or the aggregate wholesale price index (WPI), both of which combine energy costs with a wide array of food, manufactured goods, and services prices. The resulting estimates often dilute the direct fuel-price channel because the weight of fuel in headline indices is small relative to food and services. Furthermore, the CPI series with detailed component data begins only in January 2012, limiting the available sample for any study that wishes to exploit longer-term variation in oil prices, exchange rates, and pricing regimes.

This dissertation argues that the WPI Fuel and Power component is a more appropriate dependent variable for studying oil-price pass-through in India. This sub-index captures the prices of petroleum products, coal, and electricity at the wholesale level and is therefore directly exposed to imported crude oil costs. Crucially, the Office of the Economic Adviser (OEA) publishes official monthly WPI data across three successive base years—1993–94, 2004–05, and 2011–12—which, when properly chained, provide a consistent monthly series from April 1994 to the present. This yields more than three decades of monthly observations, a span long enough to cover both the administered-pricing era and the subsequent reforms that linked domestic fuel prices to international markets.

The research question addressed here is deliberately narrow:

> To what extent do rupee-denominated Brent crude oil shocks transmit to India's WPI Fuel and Power inflation, and has this transmission strengthened after the fuel-pricing reforms of 2010?

The contribution of this study is threefold. First, by focusing on WPI Fuel and Power rather than headline WPI or CPI, the analysis isolates the most fuel-sensitive layer of India's price system and avoids mixing energy pass-through with unrelated food and services price movements. Second, by constructing the rupee oil price—rather than using dollar-denominated Brent alone—the study captures the joint effect of global oil price changes and exchange-rate movements as experienced by Indian refiners and consumers. Third, the study employs Jordà (2005) local projections with Newey–West heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) standard errors, which allow flexible, model-free estimation of cumulative impulse responses at each horizon without the specification constraints imposed by vector autoregressive (VAR) systems.

The empirical strategy also allows for state dependence. India deregulated petrol pricing around June 2010, allowing retail petrol prices to move with international benchmarks rather than being held constant through government subsidies. This reform plausibly altered the speed and magnitude of oil-price pass-through to wholesale fuel prices. The local-projection framework accommodates this by interacting the oil shock variable with a post-2010 indicator, enabling a direct test of whether the response strengthened after reform.

The conceptual transmission chain underlying this study is straightforward:

> Brent USD × INR/USD → rupee oil shock → WPI Fuel and Power inflation

This chain does not require assumptions about second-round effects on core inflation or monetary policy responses. It simply tracks whether a change in the rupee cost of crude oil is reflected in the wholesale fuel price index over the following months.

The remainder of this dissertation is organised as follows. Chapter 2 reviews the background on India's fuel-pricing regime and the relevant empirical literature. Chapter 3 describes the data sources, the WPI chaining procedure, and the construction of the rupee oil price variable. Chapter 4 sets out the local-projection methodology, including the treatment of inference and diagnostics. Chapter 5 presents the main results for the all-sample and post-2010 state-dependent specifications. Chapter 6 reports robustness checks and discusses limitations. The conclusion summarises the findings and draws cautious policy implications.
