# Chapter 2: Background and Literature

## 2.1 India's Fuel-Pricing Regime

For much of the post-liberalisation period, India maintained an administered pricing mechanism (APM) for petroleum products. Under this system, the government set retail prices for petrol, diesel, kerosene, and liquefied petroleum gas, with public-sector oil marketing companies absorbing the difference between the administered price and the market-linked cost of crude oil and refining. The resulting under-recoveries were financed through a combination of government subsidies, upstream sharing by national oil companies, and oil bonds issued by the central government (Kelkar Committee, 2010). This arrangement effectively insulated domestic fuel consumers from international price volatility but imposed large and unpredictable fiscal costs.

The first significant reform came in June 2010, when the government deregulated the pricing of petrol, allowing oil marketing companies to revise petrol prices in line with international crude oil costs and the exchange rate. Diesel pricing followed in stages, with small monthly increases permitted from January 2013 and full deregulation achieved by October 2014. Kerosene and LPG remained partially subsidised, though subsidies were progressively rationalised through direct benefit transfers.

The implication for wholesale fuel price dynamics is direct. Before deregulation, administered prices meant that changes in global oil prices and the exchange rate were absorbed by the fiscal system rather than passed through to the wholesale price index. After deregulation, the WPI Fuel and Power component should, in principle, reflect international crude oil movements more quickly and more completely. This institutional shift provides the economic motivation for the state-dependent estimation strategy adopted in this dissertation.

## 2.2 WPI Versus CPI as a Measure of Fuel-Price Transmission

India publishes two principal price indices: the Wholesale Price Index (WPI) and the Consumer Price Index (CPI). The WPI measures price changes at the point of first bulk sale, while the CPI, compiled by the Central Statistics Office from January 2012 onward, measures retail prices faced by consumers.

For the purpose of studying oil-price pass-through, the WPI Fuel and Power sub-index has two advantages over headline CPI. First, the WPI Fuel and Power component has a direct compositional link to petroleum products, with crude oil and refined product prices accounting for a large share of the sub-index weight. Second, the WPI series extends back to 1994 under successive base years, providing three decades of monthly data. The CPI Fuel and Light component, by contrast, is available only from January 2012 with consistent sub-component detail, which severely limits the estimation sample and prevents meaningful analysis of the pre-reform period.

The trade-off is that WPI Fuel and Power captures only the wholesale layer and does not directly measure the prices faced by final consumers. However, for the research question posed here—whether oil shocks transmit to India's fuel price system—the wholesale layer is the economically appropriate starting point, since it is closest to the point of import and refining.

## 2.3 Oil-Shock Transmission: International Evidence

The modern literature on oil-price shocks and macroeconomic outcomes begins with Hamilton (1983), who documented the association between oil price increases and US recessions. Kilian (2009) refined this framework by distinguishing between supply-driven, demand-driven, and speculative oil price shocks, showing that the macroeconomic effects depend on the source of the price change. While this dissertation does not decompose oil shocks by source, the Kilian framework provides a useful reminder that not all oil price movements are alike and that estimated pass-through coefficients should be interpreted as average associations rather than structural causal effects.

Jordà (2005) introduced the local-projection method as an alternative to impulse responses derived from vector autoregressive (VAR) systems. Local projections estimate a separate reduced-form regression for each forecast horizon, avoiding the compounding of specification errors inherent in iterating a VAR forward. The method has become standard in applied macroeconomics for estimating cumulative responses, particularly when the researcher wishes to allow for flexible functional forms and state-dependent responses (Ramey, 2016).

## 2.4 Oil-Price Pass-Through: Indian Evidence

Several studies have examined the oil-inflation nexus in India. Mandal, Dutta, and Sarkar (2012) estimate oil-price pass-through to the WPI using a VAR framework and find significant effects at the aggregate level. Bhanumurthy, Das, and Bose (2012) examine the fiscal implications of administered pricing, documenting the large under-recoveries borne by the government during periods of high oil prices. Sarmah and Bal (2021) study the asymmetric effects of oil price changes on Indian inflation, finding some evidence of greater responsiveness to price increases. Deheri and Ramachandran (2023) provide more recent evidence on the oil–exchange-rate–inflation channel using post-2012 CPI data.

This dissertation builds on this literature by combining a long monthly WPI Fuel and Power sample, a rupee-denominated oil price measure, and a local-projection method that allows direct state-dependent estimation across the reform period. The narrower focus on WPI Fuel and Power—rather than headline WPI or CPI—is intended to sharpen the estimated pass-through by removing noise from non-fuel components.
