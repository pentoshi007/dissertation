# Dissertation PPT Viva Q&A

This file prepares you for questions an econometrics professor or external examiner may ask after seeing the PPT.

Use these answers in simple spoken language. Do not try to memorise every word. Understand the logic and speak slowly.

Core line to remember:

> Oil shocks are strong in fuel-related layers, but weak at headline CPI. This is layered attenuation, not absence of oil-price transmission.

---

# 1. Opening and main idea

## Q1. What is your dissertation about in one sentence?

**Answer:**

My dissertation studies how global oil-price shocks pass through different price layers in India, from fuel-related prices to broad consumer inflation.

## Q2. What is your main research question?

**Answer:**

The main question is: how do global oil shocks transmit across India's wholesale, retail fuel, fuel-sensitive consumer, and headline CPI layers, and where does this pass-through become weak?

## Q3. What is your main finding?

**Answer:**

The main finding is layered attenuation. Oil-price shocks pass through strongly to fuel-related layers like WPI Fuel and Power and retail petrol, but the effect becomes small and statistically insignificant at headline CPI.

## Q4. What is the contribution of your work?

**Answer:**

The contribution is that I do not look only at oil and headline CPI. I map the transmission layer by layer, using WPI, WPI Fuel and Power, retail petrol, CPI Fuel and Light, and headline CPI. This shows where the oil shock is strong and where it weakens.

## Q5. Why is this topic important for India?

**Answer:**

India imports a large share of crude oil, and crude is priced in US dollars. So India faces both global oil-price pressure and exchange-rate pressure. Oil can affect fuel prices, input costs, transport costs, and inflation. But the effect on headline CPI may be diluted.

## Q6. What is the one message you want the examiner to remember?

**Answer:**

Oil matters, but it does not affect all price indices equally. It is strong in fuel layers and weak in broad headline CPI.

# 2. Research design and layered framework

## Q7. Why do you call it a layered pass-through study?

**Answer:**

Because I study several related price layers separately. The shock is first visible near fuel prices, then it becomes weaker in broader indices. So the study is about the pattern across layers, not one single coefficient.

## Q8. Is your layered map one mechanical causal chain?

**Answer:**

No. I do not claim one mechanical WPI-to-CPI causal chain. Each layer is estimated separately. The map is an attenuation map across related price layers.

## Q9. Why did you include both wholesale and consumer price layers?

**Answer:**

Wholesale prices show upstream cost pressure, especially through WPI Fuel and Power. Consumer prices show what finally reaches the consumer basket. Including both helps show how the oil signal changes across the price system.

## Q10. Why not simply estimate Brent crude to headline CPI and stop there?

**Answer:**

That would hide the transmission path. Headline CPI alone may look weak, but fuel-related layers show strong pass-through. The layered approach explains why oil can matter even when headline CPI response is small.

## Q11. Why is the headline CPI result not the full story?

**Answer:**

Headline CPI is a broad index. It includes food, housing, services, education, health, and many non-fuel items. A weak headline CPI result does not mean oil is irrelevant. It means the oil signal becomes diluted.

## Q12. What does attenuation mean in your dissertation?

**Answer:**

Attenuation means the size of pass-through becomes smaller as the shock moves from fuel-related layers to broader headline indices.

## Q13. What is the difference between attenuation and no effect?

**Answer:**

No effect would mean oil has no role anywhere. That is not my result. I find strong effects in fuel layers. Attenuation means the effect exists, but becomes weaker before reaching headline CPI.

# 3. Data and variables

## Q14. What are your main data sources?

**Answer:**

Brent crude comes from the World Bank Pink Sheet and FRED. INR/USD comes from FRED. WPI data come from the Office of the Economic Adviser. CPI data come from MoSPI or FRED CPI sources. Retail petrol prices come from PPAC. IIP comes from MoSPI.

## Q15. Why did you use monthly data?

**Answer:**

Oil-price pass-through can happen within a few months. Monthly data are suitable because they capture short-run movements better than annual data.

## Q16. Why did you use log differences?

**Answer:**

Log differences are close to percentage changes. They also help avoid spurious regressions because price levels usually have trends, while monthly changes are more stationary.

## Q17. What does a one per cent oil shock mean in your model?

**Answer:**

It means a one per cent monthly increase in the oil-shock variable, such as rupee oil price or Brent, depending on the model.

## Q18. What is rupee oil price?

**Answer:**

Rupee oil price is Brent crude price multiplied by the INR/USD exchange rate. It measures oil-cost pressure in domestic rupee terms.

## Q19. Why use rupee oil price instead of Brent alone?

**Answer:**

Because India pays for imported crude in foreign currency. If Brent rises but the rupee appreciates, the domestic pressure is different from when Brent rises and the rupee depreciates. Rupee oil captures both Brent and exchange-rate movements.

## Q20. Why use Brent for retail petrol but rupee oil for WPI and CPI?

**Answer:**

Retail petrol pricing is closely linked to international petroleum prices, so Brent is a direct global fuel benchmark. For broader WPI and CPI models, rupee oil is more suitable because those indices face domestic oil-cost pressure after exchange-rate conversion.

## Q21. Why did you use PPAC Delhi retail petrol?

**Answer:**

PPAC provides a long and consistent monthly retail petrol series for Delhi from 2004. I use it as a retail fuel channel proxy. I do not claim Delhi alone represents every state perfectly.

## Q22. Why not use all-India petrol prices?

**Answer:**

The longest consistent monthly public series available for this purpose is the PPAC Delhi series. State prices differ because taxes differ, but Delhi gives a clear and consistent retail fuel channel.

## Q23. Why did you not use diesel also?

**Answer:**

Diesel is important, especially for transport and input costs. But I used petrol because the PPAC petrol series is consistent and directly usable for the retail fuel channel. Diesel would be a useful extension.

## Q24. Does CPI Fuel and Light directly contain petrol?

**Answer:**

Not exactly in a one-to-one way. CPI Fuel and Light is a fuel-sensitive consumer category. It includes items like fuel and light-related household expenses. I use it as a consumer fuel bridge, not as a direct petrol-only basket.

## Q25. Why use IIP as a control?

**Answer:**

IIP controls for domestic activity. If economic activity is strong, prices may rise for reasons unrelated to oil. IIP helps reduce that omitted-variable problem.

## Q26. Why use month dummies?

**Answer:**

Month dummies control for seasonality. Some prices may behave differently in certain months, and month dummies absorb those repeated seasonal patterns.

# 4. Different sample periods and comparability

## Q27. Why have you used different periods for different models?

**Answer:**

The official data series start at different dates. Headline WPI goes back much further, retail petrol starts later, and CPI Fuel and Light has a shorter available series. I used the longest reliable period available for each layer.

## Q28. If the periods are different, is the comparison valid?

**Answer:**

The main table is a layer-by-layer comparison, so I am careful not to treat every number as coming from exactly the same sample. To address this issue, I also run a common-sample consumer-chain test. In that test, the same overlapping period is used, and the attenuation pattern still appears.

## Q29. What is the most important common-sample result?

**Answer:**

In the common-sample consumer chain, CPT+ falls from 0.4007 for Brent to retail petrol, to 0.1777 for retail petrol to CPI Fuel and Light, and to 0.0064 for rupee oil to headline CPI.

## Q30. Why is the common-sample test important?

**Answer:**

It reduces the concern that attenuation is only caused by different time periods. It shows that even within a comparable sample, the pass-through falls sharply across layers.

## Q31. Can you compare WPI Fuel and Power from 2010-2026 with headline WPI from 1983-2026?

**Answer:**

Only carefully. The comparison shows that a fuel-sensitive wholesale layer has much stronger pass-through than broad headline WPI. But because the samples differ, I do not base the formal attenuation test on that comparison. The formal attenuation test is done on the common-sample consumer chain.

## Q32. Why is the title 1983-2026 if some series end earlier?

**Answer:**

The full dissertation period reflects the longest WPI coverage, from 1983 to 2026. Some consumer-side series have shorter availability, so their models use shorter samples. I mention the sample period separately for each model.

## Q33. Does using different periods weaken your study?

**Answer:**

It is a limitation, but it is handled transparently. I report the sample for each model and use the common-sample attenuation test for the key consumer-chain claim.

## Q34. Why not force all models to use only the shortest common period?

**Answer:**

That would throw away useful information from longer WPI and retail petrol series. My approach uses the longest reliable sample for each layer, and then uses a common-sample check for comparability.

## Q35. Would the results change if all models used the same period?

**Answer:**

Some exact coefficients may change, but the consumer-chain common sample still shows the same ranking: strong retail petrol pass-through, smaller CPI Fuel and Light pass-through, and weak headline CPI pass-through.

# 5. Methodology

## Q36. What model do you use?

**Answer:**

I use a short-run asymmetric ADL model. ADL means Autoregressive Distributed Lag.

## Q37. What does autoregressive mean?

**Answer:**

It means current inflation depends partly on past inflation in the same price index.

## Q38. What does distributed lag mean?

**Answer:**

It means oil shocks can affect inflation not only in the current month, but also over the next few months.

## Q39. Why use an ADL model?

**Answer:**

ADL is suitable because price adjustment takes time. It allows inflation to depend on its own past values and on current and lagged oil shocks.

## Q40. Why use a short-run model?

**Answer:**

My question is about monthly pass-through, not a long-run equilibrium relation. So a short-run ADL in monthly log differences fits the research question.

## Q41. Why not use NARDL?

**Answer:**

NARDL is usually used when studying long-run asymmetric relationships and error correction. My study focuses on short-run monthly pass-through. Using NARDL would add long-run claims that I do not need.

## Q42. Why not use cointegration or error-correction models?

**Answer:**

Because I am not modelling a long-run equilibrium between oil prices and price indices. I use stationary monthly changes and focus on short-run dynamics.

## Q43. Why not use VAR or SVAR?

**Answer:**

A VAR or SVAR could be useful for a structural shock study. But my aim is to estimate pass-through layer by layer in a transparent way. ADL keeps the interpretation simple and directly linked to each price layer.

## Q44. What is the exact ADL equation in simple words?

**Answer:**

Current inflation equals a constant, past inflation, current and lagged positive oil shocks, current and lagged negative oil shocks, controls, month effects, and an error term.

## Q45. What are positive and negative oil shocks?

**Answer:**

A positive shock is the part of the oil-price change when oil rises. A negative shock is the part when oil falls. They are entered separately so I can test whether rises and falls pass through differently.

## Q46. What is CPT+?

**Answer:**

CPT+ is cumulative pass-through for positive oil shocks. It is the sum of the coefficients on current and lagged positive shocks.

## Q47. What is CPT-?

**Answer:**

CPT- is cumulative pass-through for negative oil shocks. It is the sum of the coefficients on current and lagged negative shocks.

## Q48. Why sum the coefficients instead of reporting only one coefficient?

**Answer:**

Because the effect of oil may be spread across several months. The sum gives the total short-run effect over the lag window.

## Q49. How did you choose lag lengths?

**Answer:**

For WPI models, longer lags are used because wholesale adjustment can be slower and the data have a long monthly history. For CPI and retail petrol models, shorter lag structures are used because the focus is on faster monthly consumer-side adjustment. The choices are guided by fit, diagnostics, and economic reasoning.

## Q50. Are your coefficients estimated by OLS?

**Answer:**

Yes. The ADL equations are estimated by OLS. The inference uses Newey-West HAC standard errors.

# 6. Inference and p-values

## Q51. Why do you use Newey-West HAC standard errors?

**Answer:**

Monthly time-series residuals may have autocorrelation and changing variance. Newey-West HAC standard errors make the p-values more reliable under those problems.

## Q52. Does HAC change the coefficient estimates?

**Answer:**

No. OLS gives the same coefficient estimates. HAC changes the standard errors and p-values.

## Q53. What does a p-value mean in your results?

**Answer:**

It tells whether the estimated pass-through is statistically different from zero, under the model and standard-error assumptions.

## Q54. What p-value threshold do you use?

**Answer:**

I mainly use the 5 per cent level. I mention 10 per cent only carefully, for marginal evidence such as retail petrol asymmetry.

## Q55. What does “not statistically significant” mean?

**Answer:**

It means the data do not give strong enough evidence to reject zero pass-through at conventional levels. It does not prove the true effect is exactly zero.

## Q56. Why is headline CPI described as weak, not zero?

**Answer:**

Because the CPT+ estimate is positive, around 0.0213, but its p-value is 0.1220. So I say it is weak and not statistically significant, not zero.

## Q57. What is a Wald test?

**Answer:**

A Wald test checks restrictions on coefficients. In this dissertation, it is used to test whether cumulative pass-through is zero, whether CPT+ equals CPT-, and whether Stage 1 and Stage 3 pass-through are equal.

## Q58. What does the attenuation Wald test show?

**Answer:**

It tests whether Stage 1 pass-through equals Stage 3 pass-through in the common-sample consumer chain. The test gives F = 14.3499 and p = 0.0002, so equality is rejected.

## Q59. Why is the Wald test important?

**Answer:**

It shows that attenuation is not only a visual pattern. The fall from retail petrol pass-through to headline CPI pass-through is statistically supported.

## Q60. What does F = 14.3499 mean in simple words?

**Answer:**

It is a large enough test statistic to reject the idea that retail petrol pass-through and headline CPI pass-through are equal.

# 7. Diagnostics and model reliability

## Q61. What diagnostic tests did you use?

**Answer:**

The main diagnostic gate uses Breusch-Godfrey for serial correlation, HAC-RESET for functional form, and recursive CUSUM for stability.

## Q62. What does the Breusch-Godfrey test check?

**Answer:**

It checks whether residuals still have serial correlation. In simple words, it checks whether the model has left patterns in the errors.

## Q63. What does HAC-RESET check?

**Answer:**

It checks whether the model's functional form is reasonable. If it fails, the model may be missing nonlinear patterns or important shape features.

## Q64. What does recursive CUSUM check?

**Answer:**

It checks whether the model coefficients are stable over time.

## Q65. Why are diagnostics important?

**Answer:**

Diagnostics help decide whether a model is reliable enough to support a main claim. I do not rely on specifications that fail important diagnostic checks.

## Q66. What is your mandatory model gate?

**Answer:**

A claim-bearing model should pass BG, HAC-RESET, and recursive CUSUM. If a model fails an important gate, I do not use it for the main conclusion.

## Q67. Did all your main models pass diagnostics?

**Answer:**

The claim-bearing specifications pass the important diagnostic gate. The preferred WPI Fuel and Power model passes after using the post-2010 excluding-COVID specification.

## Q68. Why did the full-sample WPI Fuel and Power model fail?

**Answer:**

It likely mixed different pricing regimes and included COVID outliers. The full sample combines the pre-2010 administered period, the post-2010 market-linked period, and the abnormal COVID months. This created functional-form problems.

## Q69. Why did you prefer the post-2010 WPI Fuel and Power model?

**Answer:**

Because it is economically more consistent and passes diagnostics. It focuses on the period after market-linked petrol pricing became more relevant and excludes the abnormal COVID months.

## Q70. Is dropping COVID months cherry-picking?

**Answer:**

No, because the COVID months were abnormal and economically different. The rule is applied transparently and only to avoid extreme outliers distorting the preferred wholesale fuel model.

## Q71. Why exclude April to September 2020 specifically?

**Answer:**

Those months had extreme pandemic-related disruptions in oil markets, domestic demand, and pricing conditions. They are not representative of normal pass-through.

## Q72. The BG p-value for WPI Fuel and Power is close to 0.05. Is that a problem?

**Answer:**

It is something to note, but it still passes at the 5 per cent level. I therefore interpret the model carefully rather than overclaiming.

## Q73. What if a model passes diagnostics but still has omitted variables?

**Answer:**

That is possible in any reduced-form model. Diagnostics cannot prove the model is perfect. They only show that major residual and stability problems are not obvious.

# 8. Main results

## Q74. Why is WPI Fuel and Power pass-through so high?

**Answer:**

Because WPI Fuel and Power is close to the fuel and energy channel. It contains fuel-sensitive items, so oil-cost changes are more directly visible there.

## Q75. What is the WPI Fuel and Power result?

**Answer:**

In the preferred model, CPT+ is 0.5205 with p < 0.001, and CPT- is 0.4195 with p < 0.001. This shows strong wholesale fuel pass-through.

## Q76. What does CPT+ = 0.5205 mean?

**Answer:**

A one per cent positive rupee oil shock is associated with about a 0.52 per cent cumulative response in WPI Fuel and Power inflation over the model's lag window.

## Q77. Why is headline WPI much smaller than WPI Fuel and Power?

**Answer:**

Headline WPI is a broad index. It includes many non-fuel items. So the fuel signal is diluted when averaged into the full headline WPI basket.

## Q78. What is the headline WPI result?

**Answer:**

Headline WPI has CPT+ = 0.0301 with p = 0.0240 and CPT- = 0.0374 with p = 0.0012. The response is small but statistically significant.

## Q79. Why is retail petrol pass-through strong?

**Answer:**

Retail petrol is directly connected to international fuel prices and domestic fuel pricing. So a stronger response to Brent is expected.

## Q80. What is the PPAC retail petrol result?

**Answer:**

CPT+ is 0.3459 with p < 0.001, and CPT- is 0.1912 with p = 0.0002. This shows strong direct retail fuel pass-through.

## Q81. What is the CPI Fuel and Light result?

**Answer:**

CPT+ is 0.1777 with p = 0.0021. CPT- is 0.1058 with p = 0.1741. So positive retail fuel shocks pass through significantly to CPI Fuel and Light, but the response is smaller than retail petrol.

## Q82. Why is CPI Fuel and Light smaller than retail petrol?

**Answer:**

CPI Fuel and Light is a consumer basket category, not only petrol. It includes several fuel and light-related items. So the petrol signal is partly diluted inside that category.

## Q83. What is the headline CPI result?

**Answer:**

Headline CPI has CPT+ = 0.0213 with p = 0.1220 and CPT- = 0.0006 with p = 0.9375. So the headline CPI response is weak and not statistically significant.

## Q84. Why is headline CPI weak?

**Answer:**

Headline CPI is broad. Food, services, housing, education, health, and other items dominate the basket. Fuel is only one part, so the oil signal becomes small at the headline level.

## Q85. Does headline CPI being weak mean oil is unimportant for consumers?

**Answer:**

No. It means the broad CPI index does not show strong direct pass-through. Consumers may still feel fuel-price changes directly, but the headline index averages fuel with many other items.

## Q86. Is headline CPI food-heavy?

**Answer:**

Yes, CPI has a large food component in India. That is one reason fuel shocks do not dominate headline CPI.

## Q87. Why are negative shocks sometimes smaller or insignificant?

**Answer:**

Prices may adjust downward more slowly because of taxes, margins, administered elements, contracts, and pricing behaviour. But in my results, asymmetry is not the central finding.

## Q88. Did you find strong asymmetry?

**Answer:**

No. Asymmetry is not the main result. Only retail petrol shows marginal asymmetry at around the 10 per cent level. Most layers do not show statistically strong asymmetry.

## Q89. Why mention asymmetry if it is not the main result?

**Answer:**

Because oil-price increases and decreases may pass through differently, so it is important to test it. But the evidence shows attenuation is stronger than asymmetry as the main finding.

## Q90. What is the strongest result in the dissertation?

**Answer:**

The strongest result is that pass-through is large in fuel-related layers and much weaker at headline CPI. The formal attenuation test supports this.

# 9. Formal attenuation result

## Q91. What are the three stages in the common-sample consumer chain?

**Answer:**

Stage 1 is Brent to PPAC retail petrol. Stage 2 is PPAC petrol to CPI Fuel and Light. Stage 3 is rupee oil to headline CPI.

## Q92. Why does Stage 1 use Brent but Stage 3 uses rupee oil?

**Answer:**

Stage 1 focuses on direct retail petrol response to the international oil benchmark. Stage 3 focuses on broad domestic inflation, where exchange-rate conversion matters, so rupee oil is more appropriate.

## Q93. Is it valid to compare Stage 1 and Stage 3 if shock variables differ?

**Answer:**

The comparison should be read as an attenuation map across relevant layer-specific shocks, not as one identical structural shock equation. To be careful, I explain that it is not a mechanical causal chain. The main point is that fuel-layer pass-through is much larger than headline CPI pass-through.

## Q94. Why not use the exact same shock variable in every stage?

**Answer:**

Because each layer has its economically relevant shock. Retail petrol is directly linked to Brent, CPI Fuel and Light is linked to retail fuel, and headline CPI faces domestic rupee oil-cost pressure. Using layer-specific shocks makes the model more meaningful.

## Q95. What does the 63 times smaller number mean?

**Answer:**

It compares Stage 1 CPT+ around 0.4007 with Stage 3 CPT+ around 0.0064. The headline CPI response is about 63 times smaller than the retail petrol response.

## Q96. Should you say 63 times smaller in a very strong way?

**Answer:**

Say it carefully. It is a descriptive ratio from the common-sample chain. The more important formal result is the Wald test, which rejects equality between Stage 1 and Stage 3.

## Q97. What if the examiner says the chain is not fully causal?

**Answer:**

I agree. I do not claim a fully causal chain. I use it as a reduced-form attenuation map across related price layers.

## Q98. Why is the attenuation result credible?

**Answer:**

Because the pattern appears across several layers, and the common-sample Wald test rejects equality between the upstream retail petrol response and the headline CPI response.

## Q99. Could attenuation be due only to measurement differences?

**Answer:**

Measurement differences may play some role, but the economic pattern is also clear. Fuel-specific indices respond more strongly because they are closer to oil. Broad indices respond less because they average many non-fuel items.

## Q100. Could attenuation be due to policy intervention?

**Answer:**

Possibly, but my model does not prove that policy caused attenuation. Taxes, pricing choices, and administered components can affect pass-through, but I mention them only as interpretation channels.

# 10. Policy and discussion slide

## Q101. Why did you say headline CPI alone can miss fuel pressure?

**Answer:**

Because the headline CPI response is weak even when retail petrol and CPI Fuel and Light show stronger responses. So looking only at headline CPI may hide pressure in fuel-sensitive layers.

## Q102. What should policymakers monitor?

**Answer:**

They should monitor headline CPI, but also retail fuel prices, WPI Fuel and Power, and CPI Fuel and Light. These layers show fuel pressure earlier or more clearly.

## Q103. Are you giving a monetary policy recommendation?

**Answer:**

No. I am not saying the RBI should take one exact action. I am saying that oil shocks should be interpreted layer by layer, and policymakers should distinguish direct fuel shocks from wider second-round inflation.

## Q104. What do you mean by first-round and second-round effects?

**Answer:**

First-round effects are direct fuel-price increases. Second-round effects happen when fuel costs spread into transport, production costs, wages, or prices of other goods and services.

## Q105. Does your model directly estimate second-round effects?

**Answer:**

No. It does not separately identify second-round effects. I mention second-round pressure as a policy concern and interpretation point, not as a direct causal estimate.

## Q106. Why mention fuel taxes and pricing choices?

**Answer:**

Retail fuel prices include taxes, margins, and pricing decisions. These can cushion or amplify pass-through. But my model does not prove taxes caused the attenuation.

## Q107. Could high fuel taxes increase pass-through instead of reducing it?

**Answer:**

Yes, depending on how taxes are structured. A fixed tax may reduce the percentage pass-through, while an ad-valorem tax may amplify price changes. That is why I say taxes and pricing choices can change pass-through, not always reduce it.

## Q108. What is the policy message in one line?

**Answer:**

Oil pressure can build in fuel-sensitive layers before it becomes visible in headline CPI.

## Q109. Why is your policy discussion cautious?

**Answer:**

Because the model is reduced-form. It estimates pass-through patterns, not structural policy effects. So the policy discussion should guide monitoring, not prescribe a precise policy rule.

# 11. Causality and identification

## Q110. Is your study causal?

**Answer:**

It is not structural causal identification. It is a reduced-form pass-through study. I estimate associations and dynamic transmission patterns, not a fully identified causal mechanism.

## Q111. Does reduced-form mean useless?

**Answer:**

No. Reduced-form models are useful for measuring empirical relationships and pass-through patterns. They are just not the same as structural causal models.

## Q112. Can India influence global Brent prices?

**Answer:**

India is a large oil importer, but Brent prices are mainly driven by global demand, supply, OPEC decisions, geopolitics, and international financial conditions. For this study, Brent is treated as external to India's domestic price indices.

## Q113. Could reverse causality be a problem?

**Answer:**

It is less likely because Indian WPI or CPI inflation is unlikely to move global Brent prices in the short run. But I still avoid strong structural causality claims.

## Q114. Why not use instrumental variables?

**Answer:**

An IV design would require a strong instrument for global oil shocks. My study focuses on reduced-form pass-through, and Brent or rupee oil shocks are treated as external cost shocks for India.

## Q115. What would a stronger causal design look like?

**Answer:**

A stronger causal design could use structural oil-supply shocks, high-frequency event shocks, or a structural VAR separating oil supply, global demand, exchange-rate, and domestic policy shocks.

## Q116. If not causal, why use the word transmission?

**Answer:**

Transmission is used in a reduced-form sense. It means dynamic pass-through across price layers, not a fully identified structural causal path.

# 12. Robustness and limitations

## Q117. What is the main limitation of your study?

**Answer:**

The main limitation is that the models are reduced-form and the samples differ across layers because of data availability.

## Q118. What is another limitation?

**Answer:**

CPI Fuel and Light has a shorter sample, so the bridge result is based on fewer observations than headline WPI.

## Q119. Does using Delhi petrol limit the study?

**Answer:**

Yes, it is a limitation because state taxes differ. But Delhi petrol provides a consistent long retail price series, so it is useful as a retail fuel proxy.

## Q120. What robustness checks support your results?

**Answer:**

The main support comes from diagnostics, HAC inference, the common-sample attenuation test, and the fact that the attenuation pattern is consistent across related layers.

## Q121. Did you use bootstrap tests?

**Answer:**

Bootstrap symmetry checks are used as support for asymmetry testing, but they are not central to the PPT. The main result is attenuation, not asymmetry.

## Q122. Did you use Granger causality tests?

**Answer:**

They are supporting tests only. They show predictive precedence, not structural causality.

## Q123. Did you use Bai-Perron break tests?

**Answer:**

They are used as supporting evidence for breaks or regime changes, especially around the WPI fuel pricing environment. They are not the central slide result.

## Q124. Why keep some tests outside the main PPT?

**Answer:**

Because the presentation must be within 15 minutes. The main PPT focuses on the research question, method, main results, attenuation test, and policy relevance. Detailed tests can be answered if asked.

## Q125. If asked what you would improve, what will you say?

**Answer:**

I would extend the analysis using diesel and state-level fuel prices, test rural and urban CPI separately, and possibly use a structural VAR to separate global oil supply and demand shocks.

# 13. Hard examiner challenges

## Q126. Your coefficients are from different models. Can you really rank them?

**Answer:**

I rank them as a descriptive layered pattern, not as one single structural elasticity. For the strongest comparison, I use the common-sample consumer chain and a Wald test.

## Q127. Your shock variables differ. Does that weaken attenuation?

**Answer:**

It means the result should be interpreted carefully. Each model uses the shock most relevant to that layer. The conclusion is not that one identical shock mechanically travels through every equation. The conclusion is that fuel-related layers show much stronger pass-through than headline CPI.

## Q128. Why should CPI Fuel and Light be a bridge if it is not petrol?

**Answer:**

It is a fuel-sensitive consumer category, not a petrol-only category. So I call it bridge evidence. It shows that retail fuel pressure is more visible in fuel-related consumer prices than in headline CPI.

## Q129. Could food-price shocks dominate CPI and hide oil effects?

**Answer:**

Yes. That is exactly one reason headline CPI pass-through is weak. Food and other non-fuel components can dominate the headline CPI movement.

## Q130. Could oil affect food through transport costs later?

**Answer:**

Yes, but those indirect effects may be slower, smaller, and partly absorbed. My model captures overall short-run headline CPI response, but it does not separately identify each indirect channel.

## Q131. If oil affects transport, why is headline CPI not significant?

**Answer:**

Because transport is only one part of the CPI basket, and the indirect effect may be spread over time or absorbed by margins, taxes, pricing decisions, and other components.

## Q132. Could the weak CPI result be due to wrong lag length?

**Answer:**

That is possible in principle, but the lag structure is chosen based on monthly dynamics and diagnostics. Longer lags could be explored, but the present model gives a transparent short-run estimate.

## Q133. Could the weak CPI result be due to using headline CPI instead of core CPI?

**Answer:**

Core CPI would answer a different question because it excludes food and fuel. My question is about headline consumer inflation. A core CPI extension could be useful, but it is not the focus here.

## Q134. Why not include monetary policy rate as a control?

**Answer:**

Including policy rates could be useful, but it may also create endogeneity because policy reacts to inflation. My model keeps the focus on pass-through and uses activity, seasonality, and relevant policy/COVID dummies.

## Q135. Why not include taxes directly?

**Answer:**

Reliable monthly tax variation across states and products is complex. Taxes are part of the retail fuel pricing environment. I discuss them as an interpretation channel, not as an estimated causal mechanism.

## Q136. Why not include exchange rate separately in every model?

**Answer:**

For broad WPI and CPI models, the rupee oil variable already combines Brent and the exchange rate. Some specifications also include relevant controls. The aim is to measure domestic oil-cost pressure without overcomplicating the model.

## Q137. Could multicollinearity affect Brent and exchange-rate variables?

**Answer:**

Using rupee oil reduces the need to include Brent and exchange rate separately in the same equation. This keeps the shock variable interpretable as domestic oil-cost pressure.

## Q138. Are your results sensitive to base-year changes in WPI and CPI?

**Answer:**

The series are harmonised or chained where needed. Since I use log differences, the effect of base-year level changes is reduced, but careful chaining is still important.

## Q139. Why use WPI when CPI is the policy target?

**Answer:**

WPI is useful because it captures upstream price pressure. CPI is the policy target, but WPI helps show whether oil shocks appear earlier in wholesale layers.

## Q140. Is WPI Fuel and Power more important than CPI for policy?

**Answer:**

Not more important as a target. CPI remains the main consumer inflation measure. But WPI Fuel and Power is useful as an early indicator of fuel-side pressure.

## Q141. Why is asymmetry only marginal in retail petrol?

**Answer:**

Retail petrol prices may rise and fall at different speeds due to pricing behaviour, taxes, and margins. But the evidence is only marginal, so I do not make asymmetry the main claim.

## Q142. Could the positive and negative shock split create interpretation issues?

**Answer:**

It must be interpreted carefully. CPT+ and CPT- are cumulative responses to positive and negative components of monthly shocks. They are not separate structural shocks.

## Q143. Are the coefficients elasticities?

**Answer:**

They can be read like short-run percentage pass-through measures because the variables are log differences. But I call them cumulative pass-through rather than long-run elasticities.

## Q144. Does a CPT of 0.52 mean 52 per cent of the oil shock passes through?

**Answer:**

Approximately, in short-run percentage terms, yes. A one per cent positive oil shock is associated with about a 0.52 per cent cumulative response in that layer over the lag window. But it is not a long-run structural elasticity.

## Q145. Why is headline WPI significant but headline CPI not significant?

**Answer:**

WPI is closer to producer and input costs, so oil-cost pressure appears more clearly. CPI includes many consumer services and food items, so the oil signal is more diluted.

## Q146. Does inflation targeting after 2016 affect your results?

**Answer:**

It may affect the inflation environment. My reduced-form model does not separately identify the causal effect of inflation targeting. It is one reason to interpret broad-period results carefully.

## Q147. What about GST in 2017?

**Answer:**

GST may have affected price-setting and tax structure. It is part of the broader institutional background. The study focuses on oil pass-through, not on separately estimating GST effects.

## Q148. What about administered fuel pricing before deregulation?

**Answer:**

That is important. It is one reason the full WPI Fuel and Power sample is problematic, and why the preferred WPI Fuel and Power model uses the post-2010 period excluding COVID months.

## Q149. Does the model capture expectations?

**Answer:**

Not directly. Expectations may matter, but this model captures observed short-run pass-through in price data. A structural expectations channel would be a future extension.

## Q150. What if an examiner says your policy slide overclaims?

**Answer:**

I would clarify that the policy slide is about monitoring, not a causal policy prescription. The model shows where pass-through is stronger and weaker. It does not prove one exact policy action.

# 14. Slide-specific questions

## Q151. On the motivation slide, why say India is exposed to oil shocks?

**Answer:**

Because India imports crude oil and pays in foreign currency. So both global oil prices and exchange rates affect domestic oil-cost pressure.

## Q152. On the objective slide, why say “where does pass-through weaken”?

**Answer:**

Because the main question is not only whether oil matters. It is also about identifying the layer where the oil signal becomes small.

## Q153. On the conceptual framework slide, why two rows?

**Answer:**

One row shows the consumer chain and the other shows the wholesale map. This keeps WPI and CPI channels clear instead of forcing everything into one mechanical chain.

## Q154. On the data slide, why so many variables?

**Answer:**

Because each layer needs its own dependent variable and relevant shock. The study is about a system of related price layers, not one variable pair.

## Q155. On the methodology slide, what should you say if you forget the equation?

**Answer:**

Say: current inflation depends on past inflation, current and past positive oil shocks, current and past negative oil shocks, controls, seasonality, and an error term.

## Q156. On the main results slide, which row should you emphasise?

**Answer:**

Emphasise the pattern, not one row. The pattern is: WPI Fuel and Power and retail petrol are strong, CPI Fuel and Light is smaller but significant, headline CPI is weak.

## Q157. On the wholesale slide, what is the key interpretation?

**Answer:**

Oil is clearly visible in the fuel-sensitive wholesale layer, but much smaller in broad headline WPI.

## Q158. On the consumer fuel slide, what is the key interpretation?

**Answer:**

Retail petrol shows strong direct pass-through, and CPI Fuel and Light shows a smaller but significant consumer fuel response.

## Q159. On the headline CPI slide, what should you avoid saying?

**Answer:**

Do not say oil has no effect. Say the effect is weak and not statistically significant at headline CPI.

## Q160. On the attenuation slide, what is the key line?

**Answer:**

The pass-through falls sharply across the common-sample consumer chain, and the Wald test rejects equality between Stage 1 and Stage 3.

## Q161. On the policy slide, what should you avoid saying?

**Answer:**

Do not say the model proves taxes caused attenuation or that the RBI must follow one exact policy. Say the result supports monitoring fuel-sensitive layers along with headline CPI.

## Q162. On the conclusion slide, what is the final answer?

**Answer:**

Oil-price transmission is present, but it becomes diluted before reaching headline CPI.

Strong in fuel layers, weak at headline CPI.

# 16. Best final defense statement

If an examiner asks you to summarise everything, say:

> My study shows that oil shocks do pass through in India, but mainly in fuel-sensitive layers. WPI Fuel and Power and retail petrol show strong pass-through. CPI Fuel and Light shows a smaller bridge effect. But headline CPI is weak and not statistically significant. So the result is not that oil is irrelevant. The result is attenuation across layers. I use separate short-run ADL models and a common-sample Wald test to support this interpretation.
